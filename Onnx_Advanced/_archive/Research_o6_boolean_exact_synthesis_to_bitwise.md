# Exact Boolean Circuit Synthesis to Packed Bitwise ONNX

**Suggested note path:** `Research/Done/o6_boolean_exact_synthesis_to_bitwise.md` [PROMPT]

This note evaluates the narrow question K4 did not cover: whether **offline exact or near-exact boolean synthesis into XOR/AND/MAJ-style circuits**, then **transcribed to opset-18 ONNX integer bitwise operators on packed rows**, can beat the incumbent **Conv-compile** path under the project’s cost model. The short answer is **yes, but only in a narrow regime**: XOR/equality/symmetry-rich binary rules with a **cheap or amortized packer** and a **small packed-lane circuit body**. For monotone threshold/counting rules, the packed-lane bitwise route usually loses to K4’s Conv-compile or a hand Conv stack. ## Exact synthesis tooling

The practical tooling picture is stark. **General exact whole-function synthesis** is still a **small-function** technique. ABC’s recent overview says exact synthesis is “the least scalable method,” can synthesize provably smallest circuits, has been used for **all 5-input functions**, but “fails to finish on many 6-input functions.” ABC’s currently documented exact front-ends are `twoexact` and `lutexact`, taking **PLA** input or a single-output truth table on the command line; the synthesized circuit is represented as an **AIG-style netlist** and can be dumped in standard formats such as **AIGER**. That makes ABC a real exact design-time path, but not a whole-function exact solution for arbitrary 9-input or 25-input ARC window rules. [PRIMARY] citeturn13view0turn14view0turn14view1turn24search0

EPFL **mockturtle** is the most useful library for *transcribable* exact subcircuits. Its documented `exact_aig_resynthesis` uses **percy** as the underlying engine and returns an **optimum-size AIG** for the target truth table. mockturtle also packages **precomputed size-optimum** cut resynthesis for **MIGs** (`mig_npn_resynthesis`, exact up to **4 variables**) and **XAG/XMG** NPN databases up to **4 variables**. For XOR-only rules, mockturtle exposes `exact_linear_synthesis` and `exact_linear_resynthesis`, which are **SAT-based optimum XOR-network synthesis** for **XAG-like** networks. Those are the most actionable “exact XAG” paths for this card, because parity/symmetry predicates often sit almost entirely in the linear fragment. [PRIMARY] citeturn10view0turn10view1turn10view2turn9view2turn7view0

For **general XAGs**, the best exact literature path is narrower than “minimum total gate count.” Soeken’s SAT formulation for multiplicative complexity constructs XAGs with the **minimum number of AND gates** and then applies a **heuristic XOR post-optimization**. The paper reports complete optimum coverage for representatives of all **5-input affine classes** and a set of frequent **6-input functions**. That is very relevant when your target cost is dominated by AND-like structure, but it is **not** a proof of minimum total XAG size in the \{AND, XOR, NOT\} basis. [PRIMARY][SECONDARY] citeturn17view0turn10view3

For **general MIGs**, Soeken et al.’s exact MIG work is stronger: it uses **SMT-based exact synthesis** to optimize **size or depth** and says the approach is effective only for “very small functions,” specifically **up to 6 variables**. That is the cleanest exact-majority result in scope, but still well below a general **9-input whole-window** exact compile for ARC-style Moore neighborhoods. mockturtle’s packaged exact MIG path is therefore best understood as **exact cut resynthesis up to 4 vars**, with the standalone **exact_mig** research line extending practical exactness to about **6 vars**. [PRIMARY][SECONDARY] citeturn16view0turn10view2

ABC’s `if -K` and the high-effort `deepsyn` family should be classified separately. `if` is ABC’s priority-cut **LUT mapper** with optimal-delay plus heuristic area recovery; it is highly practical, but it is **not exact whole-function circuit synthesis** in the AIG/XAG/MIG sense. UCLA work describing `deepsyn` characterizes it as **high-effort optimization** based on **LUT mapping, unmapping, and subsequent AIG optimization**; again, useful, but heuristic. ESPRESSO remains important as a **PLA/SOP minimizer**, yet contemporary documentation and Berkeley distributions still frame it as a **heuristic two-level minimizer** whose natural I/O is **PLA cubes**, not XOR-rich multilevel circuits. **Bottom line for tooling.** If the target rule is an arbitrary **9-input** boolean function, there is **no practical exact whole-function XAG/MIG path** that I would recommend as the mainline design flow. The practical exact workflows are: **ABC exact AIG for ≤5 inputs**, **mockturtle exact cut resynthesis for ≤4 vars**, **Soeken exact MIG / exact-XAG-by-multiplicative-complexity around 5–6 vars**, and **mockturtle exact linear synthesis** when the rule is mostly or wholly XOR/equality structure. ## Gate mapping and cost model

ONNX gives exactly the operator family this card needs. `BitwiseAnd`, `BitwiseOr`, `BitwiseXor`, and `BitwiseNot` were added in **opset 18** and accept **integer tensors**. `BitShift` has existed since **opset 11**, but only for **unsigned integers** (`uint8/16/32/64`). In contrast, logical `And`, `Or`, `Xor`, and `Not` are limited to **bool tensors**. Therefore the packed-lane implementation should target **`uint32`** (or `uint8` where width ≤ 8), not bool, because the bitwise operators and `BitShift` are the legal packed-lane kernels. [PRIMARY] citeturn0search2turn22search5turn0search0turn21search0turn30search2turn30search0turn21search1turn21search2turn22search2

Under the project cost model, a bitwise gate costs almost entirely in the **intermediate tensor it materializes**. A unary or binary gate on a full `[1,1,30,30]` `uint8` plane costs **900 B** of memory. The same gate on a `[1,1,30,1]` `uint8` lane costs **30 B**. On the practical width-30 representation, a `[1,1,30,1]` `uint32` lane costs **30 × 4 = 120 B**. This yields the critical break-even arithmetic: **30 packed-`uint32` intermediates ≈ 1 full-plane `float32` intermediate** (`30 × 120 = 3600 B`), while **4 full-plane `uint8` bitwise intermediates ≈ 1 full-plane `float32` intermediate** (`4 × 900 = 3600 B`). So the entire packed-lane idea lives or dies on keeping the body under roughly **30 u32-lane temporaries** for width 30. [PROMPT][DERIVED]

AIG/XAG/MIG gate counts do **not** transfer 1:1 to ONNX “free” inversions. In graph-based logic representations, complemented edges are often implicit; in ONNX, unless a consumer formula absorbs polarity algebraically, you materialize an explicit `BitwiseNot` (or equivalent De Morgan rewrite). So the right conversion unit is **charged tensors**, not abstract logic nodes. One synthesized binary gate usually implies **one ONNX node and one charged output tensor**. `MAJ(a,b,c)` is not primitive in ONNX and expands to **four** bitwise ops with a good factoring, for example `maj = (a & b) | (c & (a ^ b))`, or equivalently `(a & b) | (c & (a | b))`; either way the ONNX cost is several charged temporaries even if the MIG counts one node. [DERIVED][PRIMARY] citeturn0search2turn22search5turn0search0turn21search1

The one-time adapter cost matters. If a relevant boolean source plane can be packed into a width-30 `uint32` row lane with a **cheap integer adapter**, the natural lower-bound estimate is one packed output lane (**120 B**) plus about **30 scalar weights or masks** for the row encoding. If, however, the packer must itself be synthesized from column slices, shifts, and OR trees, the adapter can consume **dozens of packed-lane temporaries** and erase much of the advantage. For this reason, the packed-bitwise regime is strongest when the source planes are **already binary**, **already reused many times**, or can be row-packed by a compact fixed adapter. [DERIVED]

A compact rule-of-thumb follows directly:

\[
\text{cost}_{u32\text{-lane}} \approx 120 \times (n_{\text{pack}} + n_{\text{shift}} + n_{\text{gate}} + n_{\text{extra-mask}}) + \text{params}
\]

This is the practical arithmetic for the current width-30 setting. For the toy width≤8 setting using a single `uint8` per row, replace `120` by `30`, and the break-even against a single full-plane `float32` intermediate jumps from about **30** temporaries to about **120** temporaries. That is why the packed idea is conceptually strongest on **narrow rows**, but still viable on width 30 if the circuit is genuinely small. [PROMPT][DERIVED]

## Crossover against incumbents

The core crossover is easy to state and hard to satisfy. K4’s sanctioned Conv-compile path already has a strong floor: one charged full-plane output tensor. If that output is `float32`, its memory floor is about **3600 B** before counting any useful parameters. A packed `uint32` bitwise body therefore wins on memory only when the total number of **materialized packed-lane temporaries stays below roughly 30**. That is a permissive budget for XOR/equality formulas and a surprisingly tight budget for threshold/counting formulas. [PROMPT][DERIVED]

For an XOR-heavy **parity CA** on one binary plane, the packed-lane body is excellent. A Moore-neighborhood parity over eight neighbors needs eight shifted neighbor lanes and only **seven XORs** in the circuit body; even with a one-plane pack and a few edge masks, the total typically lands around **2.2–2.8 kB** on `uint32` lanes. By contrast, parity is **not linearly separable**, so a single Conv+threshold over raw neighborhood bits cannot realize it directly; a two-level cover needs exponentially many parity minterms in the naive SOP view, so the Conv/SOP route expands badly. This is exactly the win regime the card hypothesized. [DERIVED][PRIMARY] citeturn9view2turn18search0turn0search1turn21search1

For a monotone threshold such as **“at least k of 8”**, the story reverses. XAGs can realize threshold by trees of adders/comparators or by majority structures, but once lowered to ONNX the packed body tends to need on the order of **two dozen or more** packed operations; if you use MIGs, every MAJ expands to several bitwise tensors. That pushes the packed-lane count to or past the **30-temporary** break-even, often **before** accounting for packers and masks. K4’s Conv-compile is a natural fit here because threshold is what Conv+threshold already computes well; this is the cleanest lose case for the bitwise route. [DERIVED][PRIMARY] citeturn16view0turn24search0

For a **corner or T-junction detector**, the outcome depends on literal complexity. A single oriented corner such as `N & E & ~S & ~W` lowers to four directional shifts, two inversions, and three ANDs; even a small rotation-merged detector typically stays around **1.2–2.5 kB** on packed `uint32` lanes after one pack. K4’s Conv-compile can still do well if the rule stays close to a compact two-level cover, but it starts from a **full-plane** memory floor, whereas the packed circuit stays in narrow row lanes throughout. This class is therefore a **real but narrower** win regime for bitwise-lane synthesis than parity. [DERIVED]

For a **small symmetry predicate**, packed XAG lowering looks unusually strong. Equality checks are `XNOR`, i.e. one XOR plus one NOT. A mirror predicate such as “left equals right and top equals bottom” maps to about **2 XOR + 2 NOT + 1 AND**, plus the required shifts. That usually lands around **1.2–2.0 kB** on `uint32` lanes, comfortably below the one-Conv memory floor. Symmetry/equality tests are precisely the kind of logic where XOR is semantically natural and Conv thresholds are awkward. [DERIVED][PRIMARY] citeturn0search1turn21search1turn0search0

The 512-entry **LUT-Gather** incumbent is not competitive in this comparison except in the narrow case already stated in the prompt: it is around **90k** and only ties a single Conv, winning mainly when a non-monotone rule would otherwise need **three or more** Conv stages. Since the packed-lane XAG body for the interesting win classes is only a few kilobytes, LUT-Gather is not the relevant challenger there. [PROMPT]

The most useful scalar summary is this: for width-30 `uint32` row packing, **bitwise-lane synthesis wins when the packed circuit body plus shift/mask overhead stays under roughly 20–25 temporaries**, is still competitive near **30**, and usually loses beyond that. XOR density lifts you into the win regime because XOR-rich functions often have **much smaller multilevel circuits** than their SOP or threshold images. Majority density pushes you out of it because **MAJ is expensive after lowering to ONNX bitwise ops**. [DERIVED]

## Design-time workflow

This entire approach is **offline design-time synthesis**, not runtime search. The synthesizer is run once on the known local rule truth table; the output is a fixed **gate netlist / Boolean chain / network object** that you then transcribe into a static ONNX graph. ABC’s recent exact-synthesis note is explicit that the exact engines are separate commands over PLA/truth-table specifications, and percy’s documentation says exact synthesis yields **Boolean chains**. mockturtle then gives you in-memory **AIG/XAG/MIG** networks and standard file writers. That is exactly the right contract for this project: run synthesis once, verify equivalence offline, and emit a constant graph with no runtime branching or search. [PRIMARY] citeturn13view0turn14view1turn7view0turn7view1

The transcribable artifact you actually want is therefore one of these:

1. an **XAG netlist** when the rule is parity/equality/symmetry heavy, ideally from `exact_linear_synthesis` if the body is linear, or from a small exact/near-exact XAG flow if the nonlinear residue is tiny; [PRIMARY][DERIVED] citeturn9view2turn17view0  
2. a **MIG netlist** only when the target is a genuinely tiny cut and majority logic is semantically compact *before* ONNX lowering; [PRIMARY][DERIVED] citeturn10view2turn16view0  
3. an **AIG/netlist** from ABC `twoexact` for very small exact subfunctions, if you then manually replace XOR-looking AIG regions with direct `BitwiseXor` during transcription. [PRIMARY][DERIVED] citeturn13view0turn14view1

The practical hazard is that **wide MAJ trees and general popcount structures destroy the packed-lane advantage after lowering**. A MIG that is compact in abstract majority logic can become bulky in ONNX because every majority node expands into several charged bitwise tensors. Similarly, if the packer cannot be implemented compactly or amortized across several downstream operations, adapter overhead can dominate. Those are the two places where an apparently elegant exact circuit fails to beat K4 in this environment. [DERIVED]

A minimal transcription sketch for the good regime looks like this:

```text
packed = PackRowU32(bool_plane)          # one-time adapter
n  = RowShift(packed, -1,  0)
s  = RowShift(packed, +1,  0)
e  = BitShift(packed, LEFT, 1) & mask
w  = BitShift(packed, RIGHT, 1)
out = BitwiseXor(BitwiseXor(n, s), BitwiseXor(e, w))
```

This is not runtime synthesis; it is just the static lowering of a preselected netlist into ONNX operators. [DERIVED][PRIMARY] citeturn21search0turn0search1turn0search2turn22search5

## Verdict

The single most actionable outcome is this: **use EPFL mockturtle’s exact linear XAG path for XOR/equality-rich binary rules, then lower to `uint32` row-packed ONNX bitwise ops**. That is the only regime where I can defend a crossover over the incumbents with straightforward arithmetic. On width-30 grids, a packed `uint32` temporary costs **120 B**, so a whole bitwise body can stay below the one-Conv memory floor as long as the total packed temporaries remain under about **30**, and the “comfortable win” region is more like **20–25**. Parity, mirror/equality, and compact non-monotone pattern predicates fit that budget; monotone threshold/counting rules do not, because either popcount/XAG logic or MAJ-to-bitwise expansion consumes too many packed tensors. So the verdict is **not** “replace K4 generally.” It is: **add one narrow compiler lane for XOR/equality/symmetry classes; keep K4 Conv-compile as the default for thresholds and generic nonlinear 3×3 rules.** [PRIMARY][DERIVED] citeturn9view2turn10view2turn16view0turn17view0turn0search1turn21search1turn0search2turn22search5turn0search0turn21search0
