# Research Card O1 — General algorithm mining for static-grid cost reducers

## Survey frame

This scan looked **outside the ONNX operator list** and focused on algorithm families whose *identities* might transfer to a fixed `30×30` discrete grid with tiny alphabets, no loops, no `NonZero`/`Unique`, and a scorer dominated by `intermediate_memory_bytes + param_elements`. The project facts that matter most are: tensors are typically `[1,C,H,W]` with `C<=10`, `H,W<=30`; values are ARC-style colors `0..9`, masks, coordinates, and counts; graph inputs and final outputs are free; params are counted only by element count; and `Loop`, `Scan`, `NonZero`, `Unique`, `Compress`, and sequence-family tricks are unavailable. `[PROMPT]`

I searched nine families because each has at least one known “compression identity” for small grids: **summed-area tables / integral images** for constant-time rectangle queries; **integral histograms** for histogram-valued rectangle queries; **rectangular morphology and sliding extrema** because separability and block-recursive algorithms often beat naive stencils; **distance transforms / min-convolutions** because thresholded distances sometimes collapse to static reductions; **rank / order filters** because ARC values live in a tiny discrete domain; **small assignment and ordered matching** because tiny object sets invite factorial-but-static exact banks; **Kronecker / tensor-train factorization** because static templates can sometimes be factorized far below dense `30×30` cost; **finite-state / weighted-automata encodings** because row/column predicates on 30 symbols can sometimes be compiled into tiny state updates; and **parallel-prefix circuit families** because scan identities sometimes matter when loops are banned. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` Crow 1984; Porikli 2005; van Herk 1992; Felzenszwalb–Huttenlocher 2012; Kuhn 1955; Burkard, Klinz, and Rudolf 1986; Van Loan–Pitsianis 1992; Oseledets 2011; Mohri 1997; Ladner–Fischer 1980; Blelloch 1990. citeturn2search1turn14search2turn3search1turn0search0turn5search1turn19search3turn8search0turn7search2turn10search2turn11search0turn11search3

Two ONNX facts made several candidates practical. First, `CumSum` is an official ONNX operator and supports both `exclusive` and `reverse`, so prefix/suffix-style constructions are first-class, not emulated. Second, ONNX has the exact primitives needed to pull rectangle corners and turn them into counts and thresholds: `OneHot`, `Gather`, `Slice`, `Pad`, `Equal`, `Greater`, `ReduceSum`, `ArgMax`, `MatMul`, `Max`, `MaxPool`, and `Cast` are all standard operators with broadcasted elementwise semantics or straightforward indexing behavior. `[PRIMARY]` citeturn1search2turn13search0turn26search2turn13search2turn25search1turn26search0turn26search1turn27search0turn12search1turn27search2turn27search4turn27search1turn27search5

## Deep dives

For the pricing below, let `N = 30×30 = 900`, `K = 10` color bins, `b` be activation bytes per element, and `M_k = (31-k)^2` be the count of valid `k×k` windows without padding. These formulas are direct consequences of the prompt’s grid bound and cost definition. `[PROMPT][DERIVED]`

**Integral histogram banks for rectangle color counts, mode, and majority.**  
Porikli’s integral histogram generalizes summed-area tables from scalars to histogram bins, giving constant-time histogram extraction for any axis-aligned region after a single prefix pass. Because ARC colors are already bounded to `0..9`, the bin count is fixed at `K=10`, so the “constant” in constant-time is tiny here. ONNX has exactly the needed operators: `OneHot` creates the bin volume, `CumSum` builds the prefix tables, and `Gather`/`Slice` plus broadcasted add-subtract recover region histograms; `ArgMax` turns those counts into exact mode/majority labels. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn14search2turn13search0turn1search2turn26search2turn12search1

For one fixed grid, the upfront graph-visible activation cost is approximately `K*N` for `OneHot` plus `2*K*N` for the two scans, i.e. `27,000 b`. Querying one rectangle then needs only four `10`-vectors and a few elementwise ops, roughly `O(10b)` visible bytes and negligible params. By contrast, the obvious static-DAG incumbent is per-query per-color masking: `Equal(X,c)` and a `900`-element rectangle mask (or equivalently a large sparse/dense template) followed by `ReduceSum`. That scales like about `900 b + 900 params` per queried color, or about `9,000 b + 9,000 params` for all ten colors of one rectangle. So the crossover is extremely early: after only a few rectangle queries on the same grid, the histogram cache is already cheaper under the scorer. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn26search0turn27search0turn14search2

The incumbent this most plausibly beats is not “plain ONNX box counting” in the abstract, but specifically **repeated rectangle-color queries on the same grid**: object bounding boxes, stripe tests, hole tests, band counts, palette comparisons, and mode/majority labels. The candidate is exact, static, and unusually well aligned with the prompt’s tiny alphabet. `[DERIVED]`

**Dual summed-area tables for sums, variance, and low-order moments.**  
Crow’s summed-area table gives constant-time rectangle sums, and later work such as Shafait et al. exploits the same identity with both `x` and `x²` to compute local mean and variance efficiently. On a `30×30` grid, that means SATs are no longer a “big-image” trick; the build itself is small. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` Crow 1984; Shafait et al. 2008. citeturn2search1turn16search0

A scalar SAT costs about `2N = 1,800` activation elements after the source image is present; adding variance means one extra `Mul(X,X)` of `900` elements and a second SAT of another `1,800`, for a total of roughly `4,500 b` graph-visible bytes. Each rectangle-stat query then touches eight scalars (four from SAT(`X`), four from SAT(`X²`)) and a handful of arithmetic ops. The practical baseline is a per-query dense mask or per-query slice-reduce over the full window: one masked sum for `X`, one masked sum for `X²`, and one `900`-element rectangle mask if the window is static. That is about `1,800 b + 900 params` per rectangle statistic request. So this candidate wins whenever the same grid supports several rectangle-stat queries, or when multiple box features share the same SATs. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn2search1turn16search0turn27search0

Relative to current NeuroGolf-style incumbents, this is best thought of as a **shared statistics cache** for any task built from box sums, area-normalized means, rectangular mass moments, or variance thresholds. It is exact and does not require any banned control flow. `[DERIVED]`

**Exact rectangular rank filters on `0..9` via histogram counts, not sorting.**  
The classic fast median-filter papers by Huang et al. and Perreault–Hébert replace sorting with histogram maintenance. The user’s domain improves on their setting because the value range is known and tiny: there are only ten possible values. That means the best transfer is *not* a generic running histogram with scan dependencies, but a static ONNX construction that computes window histograms and then recovers any rank by cumulative counts over the ten bins. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` Huang et al. 1979; Perreault–Hébert 2007. citeturn18search3turn18search0

For a `7×7` rectangular median over the whole `30×30` grid, the integral-histogram build is the same `27,000 b`. The output window-count volume then has `K*M_7 = 10×576 = 5,760` elements; four-corner extraction for the whole map is about `4×5,760 = 23,040` elements, and the bin-prefix / threshold / `ArgMax` recovery adds another order of `10×576`. So the total exposed activation budget is on the order of `60,000 b`, with negligible params. The direct baseline that stacks all `49` neighbors for each window already needs `49×576 = 28,224` visible elements **before** any selection logic, and a generic compare-sort route scales badly beyond `3×3` or `5×5`. For larger rectangular windows, histogram-valued logic is therefore the first exact method that looks plausibly scorer-friendly. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn14search2turn18search3turn18search0turn12search1turn26search1

The important caveat is exactness. This construction is exact only because the prompt constrains values to a tiny discrete alphabet. `[PROMPT][DERIVED]` On unrestricted numeric tensors, generic selection networks or `TopK`-style routes become more competitive for very small windows. So the verdict is: **excellent for large rectangular rank filters on ARC colors; not clearly better than hand-tuned `3×3` selection networks for tiny windows.** `[DERIVED]`

**Exact tiny-object assignment by permutation banks.**  
Kuhn’s assignment formulation is static: for `n` items per side the objective is the minimum over `n!` permutation sums. On normal-sized problems that is useless; on ARC-style problems where the relevant object count is often tiny, it becomes a very plausible static-DAG primitive. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` Kuhn 1955. citeturn5search1

For `n=4`, a full bijective matcher needs only `4! = 24` permutations. If the graph already has or can cheaply derive a `4×4` pairwise cost matrix, then a constant permutation bank stores `24×4 = 96` indices, `Gather`s the corresponding entries, reduces each length-4 sum, and takes a `ReduceMin`. Even with pairwise `dx`, `dy`, and Manhattan or squared-distance costs, the visible intermediary sizes are only in the dozens to low hundreds of elements. For `n=5`, the bank is `120×5 = 600` param elements, still tiny relative to a single dense `30×30` template. This is one of the few ways to get **exact collision-free correspondence** without loops. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn5search1turn26search2turn27search0

The incumbent this beats is any loop-free surrogate that does **not** enforce a bijection: row-wise nearest-neighbor, greedy matching hardcoded in graph structure, or soft correspondence scores. `[DERIVED]` If the geometry is known to be ordered on a line and the cost is Monge/convex, the search can shrink even further to order-preserving pairing, using the Monge structure studied by Burkard, Klinz, and Rudolf and related line-transport results. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn19search3turn28search1

**Kronecker-separable and TT-factorized static templates.**  
Van Loan and Pitsianis show that matrices can be represented as sums of Kronecker products by an SVD-like construction on a permuted matrix, while Oseledets’ tensor-train decomposition gives a stable chained factorization for higher-order reshapes. These are not the same as CP-rank cards; they target row/column separability and multiway chain structure rather than sums of outer products over the original axes. `[PRIMARY]` citeturn8search0turn7search2

For the NeuroGolf cost model, the key derived identity is simple. A dense static `30×30` template score `⟨M, X⟩` costs `900` params. If `M = uv^T` exactly, the same score is `u^T X v`, which ONNX can do with two `MatMul`s using only `30 + 30 = 60` param elements and a single visible intermediate of length `30`. A rank-`r` Kronecker sum uses `60r` params instead of `900`. That is a `15×` parameter reduction already at rank `1`, and it stays attractive for small `r`. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` ONNX `MatMul`; Van Loan–Pitsianis 1992. citeturn27search2turn8search0

This is strongest for **static masks, coordinate ramps, stripe detectors, rectangle-edge templates, and template banks** that are exact or near-exact sums of row/column factors. It is weaker for arbitrary bitmaps, where the Kronecker rank may approach the dense case. TT-style reshapes may still rescue some of those cases, especially when a template bank shares repeated local motifs, but that needs repo-side probing because the useful reshape/factor choices are highly library-dependent. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED][SPECULATIVE]` citeturn8search0turn7search2

## Dead ends

The biggest attractive dead end was **fast rectangular morphology in the van Herk / Gil–Werman style**. The primary sources are real and important: van Herk’s algorithm makes local min/max filtering use a constant number of comparisons per pixel independent of kernel size, and later variants generalize that idea. But ONNX already has `MaxPool`, which expresses rectangular max-filtering directly with kernel shape, stride, and padding as operator attributes rather than graph-visible tensors. Under the user’s scorer, that means the algorithmic comparison-count win is largely invisible: the output feature map is the same size regardless of whether the backend internally uses naive pooling or van Herk-style scanning. So this family is a **CPU-time optimization, not a graph-cost optimization** here. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn3search1turn27search1turn20search0

A related dead end was **exact Euclidean or L1 distance transforms**. Felzenszwalb–Huttenlocher give elegant linear-time algorithms based on lower envelopes of shifted convex functions, and the paper explicitly connects these transforms to min-convolution and grayscale morphology. But the state carried by the envelope construction is exactly the kind of scan-like, data-dependent structure that is awkward to encode without loops or a very large explicit unrolling. The only part that transfers cleanly is the special “box-distance” case, which is effectively a min/max filter and therefore collapses back to pooling logic. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn0search0turn0search47turn27search1

**Medial-axis and skeleton ideas** were also less transferable than they first appear. The ridge/singularity information that makes skeletons useful also makes them brittle; even continuous-shape papers emphasize instability and the need for pruning or additional regularization. In the present contract that instability combines badly with the need for an exact DT, tie logic, and topology-sensitive pruning, none of which map cleanly to small static ONNX DAGs without either banned ops or many hardcoded heuristics. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` citeturn17search0turn17search1

Two more families were searched and mostly declined. **Parallel-prefix circuit variants** are theoretically elegant, but ONNX already exposes `CumSum`, so replacing one official scan op with a hand-built prefix tree only changes depth, and depth is not part of the stated cost. `[PRIMARY][DERIVED]` **Finite-state / weighted-automata row scanners** are intellectually plausible on 30-symbol rows, but the lack of repo access means I could not verify that there are enough row/column-grammar tasks to justify a new primitive; for now I would treat them as a narrow follow-up rather than a front-line optimization card. `[PRIMARY][SPECULATIVE]` citeturn11search0turn11search3turn10search2turn22search3

## Final verdict and checklist

The single most promising new research direction is **integral-histogram caching on the fixed 10-color alphabet**, not generic morphology, not generic distance transforms, and not another ONNX-operator sweep.
