## Cost-model pluggability

[PROMPT] Your scorer is `25 - ln(memory + params)`, with `memory` equal to the sum over **chosen node outputs** and `params` equal to the bytes of all selected initializers. [DERIVED] That makes extraction a **weighted DAG** problem, not a tree problem: shared subexpressions and shared initializers must be charged once, while multi-output nodes must charge all reachable selected outputs exactly once. [DERIVED] A greedy tree extractor will mis-score this objective whenever common subexpressions or common constants matter. citeturn12view3turn46view0turn20search0turn24view2

[PRIMARY] TENSAT is the clearest proof that greedy extraction is not enough for tensor graphs: its paper compares greedy extraction with ILP extraction and shows real cases where greedy is worse, because it cannot see the value of shared right-hand-side subgraphs. [PRIMARY] The 2024 tensor-eqs+MCTS follow-on makes the same criticism more explicitly: cost functions that sum child costs overestimate graphs with shared common subexpressions. [PRIMARY] The `egg` maintainers likewise describe default extraction as tree-optimal and LP extraction as DAG-optimal, and note that DAG extraction is NP-hard in general. citeturn12view3turn46view0turn20search0turn10search14

[PRIMARY] In raw `egg`, a user-supplied cost function is straightforward: `CostFunction` handles greedy extraction, and the project exposes LP/ILP-style cost traits/extractors for DAG-aware objectives. [SECONDARY] Newer extraction work broadens the menu: OOPSLA 2024 gives an exact sparse/treewidth algorithm for low-treewidth e-graphs; the 2023/2024 “acyclic constraints” work improves exact extraction by replacing topological-order encodings with cycle-aware MAXSAT/ILP encodings; SmoothE 2025 supports differentiable non-linear cost models but is heuristic rather than exact; e-boost 2025 is hybrid heuristic+exact, also not guaranteed globally exact in the general case. [PRIMARY][SECONDARY] citeturn20search14turn20search0turn26view0turn24view2turn24view1turn24view3

[PRIMARY] In `egglog`, the situation is weaker today. The paper says it can extract optimized terms, but the maintainers’ own 2024 issue states that extraction is currently tree-based and assumes static function costs; the Python layer adds per-value `set_cost`, but “custom DAG extraction” is still being pushed through serialization hooks rather than a mature built-in API. [PRIMARY][SECONDARY] So `egglog` is attractive for prototyping analyses and guards, but `egg` is the better substrate if the extraction objective is the heart of the project. citeturn17view0turn22view0turn23view0

[DERIVED] For **your** cost, the right exact objective looks like this for a chosen extracted DAG:  
`total_cost = Σ(selected_node_outputs) bytes(output_i) + Σ(selected_unique_initializers) bytes(init_j)`. [DERIVED] That is naturally representable as a 0-1 optimization problem with one binary variable per candidate e-node (or per candidate output-producing e-node where needed), plus reachability / acyclicity constraints, plus extra binaries to charge each initializer once. [DERIVED] This is a very good fit for the same exact-extraction family TENSAT and later e-graph extraction papers already use. citeturn12view3turn24view2turn26view0

[PROMPT] Your `memory` definition uses `max(static_bytes, runtime_bytes)` where `runtime_bytes` come from ONNX Runtime profiling on real inputs. [DERIVED] Existing equality-saturation frameworks do **not** natively model ONNX Runtime’s profiler fields. [DERIVED] If those runtime bytes ever differ materially from simple dtype×shape byte counts in your scorer, then the extractor can optimize only a compile-time proxy and you need a final outer-loop oracle: extract candidate → serialize ONNX → score with your real validator/profiler → keep if improved. [SPECULATIVE] For tiny static graphs, that outer loop is perfectly plausible for local search, but expensive for deep equality-saturation exploration. citeturn42view1turn43search2

## Rewrite-rule sources

[PRIMARY] TASO’s published rule source is operator-specification driven: it enumerates graph pairs over its tiny tensor algebra, filters candidates with randomized tests, and proves them from 43 operator properties. [PRIMARY] Its core operator family is small and heavily centered on Conv/Pool/MatMul/ReLU/Concat/Split/Transpose. [PRIMARY] TENSAT inherits that lineage and adds multi-pattern rewriting plus a larger repo-level op table; the public `op_table.txt` includes Conv, MatMul, Pad, Slice, Resize, Cast, comparisons, `Where`, `TopK`, `Not`, and specific reductions, but still omits a large fraction of your ONNX contest surface. [DERIVED] In particular, I found no built-in support there for `Gather`, `ScatterElements`, `ScatterND`, `And`, `Or`, `Xor`, `Tile`, `Expand`, `OneHot`, `CumSum`, `Trilu`, `QLinearConv`, or ONNX-native `Einsum`. citeturn38view0turn33view1

[DERIVED] That is a strong signal that the post-TENSAT tensor-egraph line is still mostly optimizing the same Conv/MatMul-centric algebra, not moving toward ONNX’s uncommon indexing, scatter, and sequence-style operators. citeturn46view0

## Equivalence guarantees

[PRIMARY] TASO’s guarantee is the strongest among the tensor-graph frameworks I reviewed, but it is still not the guarantee you need. It generates candidates by randomized testing and then proves them from operator properties with Z3. [DERIVED] That is excellent for “semantic equality under the user-specified tensor algebra,” but it is not a bit-level proof under ONNX + IEEE-754 + opset-specific edge cases + arbitrary floating initializers. [DERIVED] TENSAT and the 2024 MCTS follow-on inherit this caveat because they reuse the TASO/TENSAT rewrite lineage. citeturn13view0turn36view3turn12view1turn46view0

[DERIVED] For your use case, the clean classification is this:

| framework family | exact for your contest | exact only under extra integer-exactness assumptions | approximate / unusable as-is |
|---|---|---|---|
| TASO / TENSAT / tensor-eqs+MCTS | Structural/index-preserving rewrites only: no-op reshape/transpose collapse, exact `Concat`/`Split` inverses with static partitions, zero-`Pad` fused into `Conv`/`Pool`, dead-node elimination. [DERIVED] | Most algebraic Conv/MatMul/Add/Mul rules at best. [DERIVED] They are safe only if every intermediate remains integer-exact and the rewrite does not introduce non-power-of-two coefficients or change rounding-sensitive accumulation. [DERIVED] | Many published “good for runtime” rules. [DERIVED] Example: `AvgPool(3x3)` ↔ depthwise `Conv` with `1/9` weights is not bit-exact because `1/9` is not exactly representable in binary floating point. [DERIVED] |
| egg / egglog / DialEgg / eqsat / ONNX-native rewriters | Yes, if **you** only encode exact structural rules and legality guards. [PRIMARY][DERIVED] | Yes, for arithmetic rules only under explicit magnitude-and-coefficient guards. [DERIVED] | Yes, if you import generic floating-point algebra rules naively. [DERIVED] |
| Glenside / STENSO / Prism | Exact in their own DSL semantics. [PRIMARY] | At best, when you separately prove the DSL rewrite preserves ONNX/IEEE behavior after lowering. [DERIVED] | Off-the-shelf use on ONNX graphs is unsafe. [DERIVED] |
| PET | No. [PRIMARY] | No. [PRIMARY] | Yes: PET is intentionally partially equivalent before correction. [PRIMARY] |

[DERIVED] The integer-exactness rule of thumb you asked for is: **float32 preserves every integer exactly only up to `2^24 = 16,777,216`; float16 preserves every integer exactly only up to `2048`.** [DERIVED] Therefore, reassociation rules such as associativity/linearity become safe only when every rewritten intermediate stays inside those bounds and no division/rounding/non-integer coefficient is introduced.

[DERIVED] Proof sketch for the safe class: pure data-movement rules (`Reshape`, `Transpose`, `Slice`, `Concat`, `Split`, `Pad` with zero fused into an op that already defines zero-padding) do not change numerical arithmetic at all; they only change indexing or remove redundant materialization, so the tensor elements computed at each reachable output index are identical bit-for-bit. [DERIVED] For boolean and integer ops (`Equal`, `Greater`, `Less`, `Not`, `Where` over a guard, exact `ArgMax` monotonicity conditions), exactness holds when the rewrite preserves the same element comparisons and does not change tie-breaking or axis semantics.

[DERIVED] Counterexample for “real-arithmetic-only” associativity: in float32, `((2^24 + 1) + 1)` evaluates to `2^24`, while `(2^24 + (1 + 1))` evaluates to `2^24 + 2`; so naive reassociation is **not** bit-exact. [DERIVED] Counterexample for “approximate” cancellation: `(x / 3) * 3` at `x = 1.0f` is not exactly `1.0f`. [DERIVED] Counterexample for coefficient synthesis: replacing a 3×3 average pool by a convolution with all weights `1/9` is not bit-exact because `1/9` is not exactly representable in binary floating point.  

[DERIVED] The most important practical consequence is this: **integer-valued inputs do not rescue runtime-oriented Conv/MatMul algebra if the graph’s initializers contain arbitrary floats.** Even with reachable inputs in `{0,1}`, rules that reorder sums/products against non-integer weights can still change rounding. That single fact sharply reduces how much value you can safely recover from TASO/TENSAT’s built-in rule families. citeturn38view0turn33view1

## Constraint handling

[PROMPT] Your validator bans `Loop`, `Scan`, `NonZero`, `Unique`, `Script`, `Function`, `Compress`, any `model.functions`, any non-default domain, any GRAPH/GRAPHS attribute, and any symbolic dimension. [DERIVED] This is a good fit for ONNX-native tooling and for custom e-graph languages that simply never admit those constructs; it is a poor fit for frameworks whose lowering path goes through richer compiler IRs and then tries to come back later. citeturn42view0turn43search2turn43search6

[PRIMARY] TENSAT already does shape checking before applying rewrites. [DERIVED] So shape/static-dimension legality can clearly be encoded in an equality-saturation workflow. [DERIVED] But TENSAT is not ONNX-native, and its type/opset/domain legality story is inherited from TASO’s tensor algebra rather than from ONNX’s actual validator surface. ## Port-effort estimate

[DERIVED] The single best **technical** candidate is: **`egg` as the extractor/search engine, with an ONNX-native front/back end built using `onnx-ir` or ONNX Script’s IR utilities, and with an exact-rule library restricted to structural/data-movement identities first.** [DERIVED] This is better than porting TENSAT wholesale because TENSAT is already entangled with TASO’s cost model, op algebra, and CUDA-oriented environment; raw `egg` lets you keep only the part you actually need: e-classes, guarded rewrites, and custom DAG extraction. citeturn20search14turn20search0turn43search2turn42view0turn28view0

[DERIVED] The integration sketch looks like this:

```text
1. Load model with onnx_ir / ONNX Script IR.
2. Reject immediately if banned ops, functions, graph attrs, or symbolic dims appear. [PROMPT]
3. Lower each ONNX value-producing node to an egg language term:
   op(name, attrs_canonicalized, inputs..., shape, dtype, opset, init_id?)
4. Represent multi-output ops as explicit projection nodes:
   split_i(split(...)), topk_values(topk(...)), topk_indices(topk(...)).
5. Attach e-class analysis:
   - static shape
   - dtype
   - legality flag
   - output byte size
   - reachable initializer-id set
6. Run only exact structural rewrites first.
7. Extract with exact DAG optimization:
   self_cost(enode) = Σ output_bytes(enode.outputs) + fresh_initializer_bytes
   subject to legality + acyclicity + root reachability.
8. Rebuild ONNX.
9. Score with your real validator/oracle; keep only improving, exact candidates.
```

[DERIVED] A worked micro-example that this style of system should catch reliably is **zero-`Pad` + `Conv` fusion**:

```text
Original:
  p = Pad(x, pads=[0,0,1,1,0,0,1,1], mode="constant", value=0)
  y = Conv(p, W, pads=[0,0,0,0])

Rewrite:
  y = Conv(x, W, pads=[1,1,1,1])

Guard:
  Pad mode is constant-zero, no negative pads, Conv auto_pad unset/compatible.
```

[DERIVED] Why exact: ONNX `Conv` with explicit `pads` is defined as zero-padding the input tensor before convolution; a separate constant-zero `Pad` node followed by a zero-pad `Conv` computes the same tensor values index-for-index. [DERIVED] Why the cost likes it: on your fixed input shape `[1,10,30,30]` float32, the intermediate `Pad` output has shape `[1,10,32,32]`, which costs `1*10*32*32*4 = 40,960` bytes. [DERIVED] The `Conv` output is unchanged in both graphs. [DERIVED] So this rewrite drops `memory + params` by exactly `40,960` bytes with no parameter change, improving the per-task score by `ln((old_total)/(new_total))` points. [PROMPT][DERIVED] This is exactly the kind of win an equality-saturation search can preserve while exploring interacting structural rewrites around it. citeturn40view0turn42view0

[DERIVED] The five hardest obstacles are:

1. **Semantic rule authoring, not the e-graph engine.** Existing tensor-superoptimizer rules are mostly too floating-point-aggressive for your validator. You will spend most of the time authoring/guarding a safe exact subset.  
2. **Multi-output ONNX ops.** `Split`, `TopK`, and similar nodes force an SSA/value-oriented encoding rather than a naïve tree encoding.  
3. **Exact DAG extraction under your byte objective.** Greedy extractors are wrong here; you need ILP/MAXSAT/LP-style extraction or a sparse exact extractor.  
4. **Initializer charging and ONNX export legality.** The extractor must count each unique initializer once, preserve default-domain opsets only, and avoid generating illegal attributes or symbolic dims.  
5. **True-score reconciliation.** If your profiler-derived `runtime_bytes` ever disagree with compile-time byte estimates, you need an outer validation loop.

[DERIVED] My effort grade for one experienced engineer is **XL** for a broad, competition-grade equality-saturation port, and **L** only for a deliberately tiny prototype over the safest 10–20 structural rule schemas.

## Overall verdict

[DERIVED] **Skip a 1–2 week port of TASO, TENSAT, or any other off-the-shelf equality-saturation tensor framework.** The only existing framework technically worth building on is raw `egg`, but a competition-grade ONNX pipeline around it is an **XL** project because the hard part is not saturation; it is writing a safe exact ONNX rule set, modeling multi-output SSA values, and implementing a DAG-aware byte-cost extractor that respects your validator. [DERIVED] For your actual time horizon, the rational choice is **hand-optimization plus a homegrown ONNX-native peephole/search pass** using `onnx-ir`, ONNX Script Rewriter, and the ONNX Optimizer pass catalogue as scaffolding. Restrict the first wave to structural, shape-guarded, obviously exact rules (`Pad→Conv/Pool`, `Concat/Split/Slice`, `Transpose/Reshape`, `Shape/Gather`, duplicate-initializer and dead-node cleanup), score every candidate with your real oracle, and only consider an `egg` backend later if that exact peephole inventory starts to bottleneck. citeturn20search14turn20search0turn42view0turn40view0turn43search2
